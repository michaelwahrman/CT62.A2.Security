An experimental security console for a synagogue: venue and camera layout, a people database with photos, duty officers and alert procedures, and a simulation of people arriving. Cameras take still frames at 1-2 fps, a stand-in "recogniser" matches faces, and rules raise alert levels.

Status: prototype (art-style rules, assumptions written down, strict rules to come later). Everything here is fictional: the building, the people, the phone numbers and the face pictures. No real face recognition is done and nothing is sent to anyone. Notifications appear on screen marked SIMULATED.

Running it

Open index.html in a browser (Chrome, Edge or Firefox). It works straight from disk. three.js r186 is bundled in lib/, so nothing is downloaded.

To edit the JSON files and see the changes without rebuilding, serve the folder instead:

python3 -m http.server 8762

and open http://localhost:8762/. When the page is opened from disk it reads data/bundle.js. Rebuild that file after editing data/*.json:

python3 scripts/bundle_data.py

The information panels are off when the page opens (so they don't cover the view on a phone). Press Panels or the P key to show them.

Zones and alert levels

Zones: 0 outside (streets, and the grounds inside the fence), 1 entry (forecourt from the gate to the doors, and the lobby), 2 interior (sanctuary, classroom, and the office, which is marked staff only).

Rules: threat-list match (3), no longer trusted match (2), unrecognised person in zone 1 for more than 8 s (1), unrecognised and uncleared person inside (2), unrecognised person loitering outside for 240 s (1), known person outside open hours (1), person in a zone they aren't allowed in (1), object of concern (2), and object plus an unrecognised person in zone 1/2 (3). All of these are editable in alerts.json or on the Duty tab. They are placeholder procedures, not reviewed security advice.

How the simulated recogniser works

For each camera frame, each person is tested against the camera's view pyramid. Walls block the view (2-D line of sight; walls are assumed taller than the cameras). The estimate is then:

  • face width in pixels = 0.16 m / (2 x distance x tan(HFOV/2)) x horizontal resolution
  • pixel quality = 0 below 24 px, rising to 1 at 64 px
  • angle quality = 1 frontal (< 30 deg), 0.85 three-quarter (< 60), 0.6 profile (< 100; we hold side photos), 0 from behind
  • an enrolled person is matched with probability 0.97 x pixel quality x angle quality. A person who is not enrolled

is never matched (no false matches in A1).

  • a carried object is noticed with probability 0.5 per frame once the person is 160 px or taller in the frame

One worked number: a 90 deg camera at 2560 px gives about 20 px of face at 10 m. That's too small to match, which is why the street cameras catch passers-by but don't identify them.

!Face coverage map, red = no usable face view on the property

Finding from the sample layout: with 10 cameras at 2560x1440, usable face coverage exists only along the entry path (gate, forecourt, doors, lobby, sanctuary doors). No spot on the property is covered by 3 or more cameras, and the seating area, the grounds and the classroom are blind spots for faces. Someone facing the ark from the bimah is never identified, so the arrivals scenario has the rabbi walk in through the lobby. The coverage map is meant for exactly this kind of planning.

Assumptions (UNVERIFIED, to be checked before any real use)

  1. Face-size thresholds (24 px usable, 64 px reliable, 0.16 m face width) are planning guesses. Real thresholds

depend on the recognition model, lighting, motion blur, compression and lens distortion. Check them against the chosen camera and model.

  1. Wide-angle lenses are modelled as plain perspective cameras. Barrel distortion and the drop in resolution at

the edges are ignored.

  1. Tracking hand-off is perfect. The same person keeps one track across all cameras, and the zone comes from their

true position. In a real system, re-identification across cameras is a hard problem in its own right.

  1. No false matches, and no look-alikes. Real systems have false-match and false-non-match rates that must be

measured.

  1. Object detection is a coin flip once the person is large enough in the frame. It is not a model of any real

detector.

  1. Low light: night frames are shown greyscale ("IR"), but recognition quality is not reduced at night.
  2. Frame timing: each camera takes frames at its own fps with a fixed phase. There is no network delay and no

lost frames.

  1. Procedures, notification lists and level names are placeholders. They are not reviewed advice from police,

security professionals or community security organisations.

  1. Privacy and law: holding photos of members, and especially of people judged to be threats, raises

biometric-privacy, consent and data-retention questions that differ by country and state. They have not been researched here and must be before real deployment.

Tests

python3 scripts/run_selftest.py

This runs the data check, 11 camera-model checks, and the 7 scenarios with 5 random seeds each (35 runs). See docs/TEST_REPORT.md. Last result: PASS (11/11 checks, 35/35 runs). These tests show the program does what it was written to do. They do not show that real cameras or recognition would behave this way.
